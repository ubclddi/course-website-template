## 2) Assignment 2 (20%): Close Reading and Comparative Analysis

Reading *The AI Mirror* (Introduction, and chapters 1 and 2) in conversation with a piece of fiction.Choose from one of: *Frankenstein* (1818/1831) (the novel!), *Klara and the Sun* (2021), *Metropolis* (1927), or *Bladerunner* (1982).

In MET, we spend a lot of time talking about all kinds of technologies and media, both in the context of education and outside of it. One topic we cover less, but that is vital for understanding and navigating our current moment with AI/GenAI, is properly accounting for and grappling with *literacy* and *writing* as technologies. Reading and writing are a practice, they are a technology, one we often take for granted, increasingly so in an era of grammar checkers and LLM story-weavers. So, you are tasked here, in this assignment, to try your hand at some weighty close-reading and writing. Read the listed sections of *The AI Mirror* and read/watch the second, fictional story of your choice. Then, write a medium-length (1500-2000 word) comparative analysis that identifies some of the ways that these stories talk about people (us), media, technologies, knowledge, ethics, morals, justice, responsibility.

The challenge here for you is straightforward, but will be difficult, and that is to **stay in the text** that you are reading. If you choose *Frankenstein* for example, your work should aim to engage with the questions you are exploring in your work (and some are also included below to help get you started) by pulling ONLY from *Frankenstein* and from *The AI Mirror*. Good, careful, and critical reading requires us to be able to stay in the text! The more we drift from it, because we remember something from work that an idea in the book draws us towards, or from a memory that it reminds us of, or a conversation with a friend or a partner; or, because our phone send us another push notification that knocks our focus off balance, the further we move away from the text and less we are able to engage meaningfully with it. You don't have to answer every question listed below, and you may well have your own that you find important to pursue in your work, just try, as best you can, to stay within Vallor and your chosen text. As you read, pay attention to the core concepts, ideas, arguments, and examples/places where Vallor and your chosen story connect. At the same time, see if you can locate some examples of where these connections fray or break down, and think what that might be able to tell us.

Some questions you might consider including in your work include: How does fiction help us to understand human relationships with technologies like GenAI? How might Vallor's work on AI/GenAI provide new and productive ways of understanding these fictional stories? What is important for us to know about the ways that human/technology and human/media relationships are introduced and understood in Vallor and your chosen fictional text? What moral or ethical questions are posed? What boundaries are crossed? How is responsibility assigned (or shirked)? What is your chosen story/Vallor trying to tell us about our 'human-ness' and about our media/technology? In terms of teaching and learning, how are education and knowledge framed in these texts? Who/what is responsible for our epistemological wellbeing? What are the limits of the metaphors these texts invoke or conjure? Where do the metaphors break?

Have fun with this assignment, and GOOD LUCK.

## 3) Project Assignment (40%): 2 Options

### Option A

Option A for the major assignment for this course, broken into several parts, will involve working **in groups** and using GenAI to devise a learning module that is centered around a specific subject and learner cohort. You will decide which subject and target user you wish to develop this module for, and which GenAI tool(s) you will use to assist in design and development. As your group navigates this activity, describe your use of GenAI tools with reference to accuracy and reliability, potential biases, ethical and pedagogical implications. The final submission will consist of a 'proof of concept' for the designed learning module.

#### Part 1a

By the end of week 3, find/make a group to work with

#### Part 1b (5%): Selection of GenAI tools and development of an evaluation framework for assessing genAI use and performance

For the first part of this assignment, produce a brief mockup (1-2 pages) that outlines the specific GenAI tools you are thinking about or intend to use for your project and explains potential uses and limitations of each tool. Reflect upon the unique ethical and applicative questions that are raised by each specific GenAI you have identified, paying close attention both to what these tools can offer, and the critical questions they may raise about data security, learning design, user (student) participation, assessment, curricular goals, and sociocultural/algorithmic bias.

#### Part 2 (10%): Proposal

After completing the first stage of the assignment, move on to assembling a formal proposal for your work (~1200 words). There is no one-size-fits-all template for this proposal, though there are some major points that you will be asked to speak about in your work, which are noted below. You can use whatever format/presentation style works best for your group, and multimodality is welcome for this task. In addition to these listed points, you may include anything else that you view as important to know about your project.

1. A name or title for your proposed project, which could be related to the topic that will be centered in your refusal framework.
2. A breakdown of group members and member roles as they are understood at this point
3. The subject matter and learner cohort you have chosen for your 'proof of concept' learning module, along with a very brief (i.e. short paragraph) description of what has led you to make these choices in terms of content.
4. What platform your group will be using to develop this module (i.e. Google Sites, Genially, Canva, Prezi, Figma, Fectar, among many others). We are *design agnostic*, so you should feel empowered to explore and decide upon a platform that you believe will fit well with the scope of your project.
5. What GenAI tools your group intends to use to assist in the design and development process. In addition, in as much detail as you can offer, *how* you intend to integrate them into your work. What opportunities and challenges do the integration of GenAI raise for your project? Take some time here to expand upon the work you did in part 1 RE: the ethical and applicative questions you introduced.
6. Any information you can offer regarding design and use goals for your learning module. What are you intending for or hoping for users to do with this? What are you hoping they learn via interacting with it? Are you aligning your learning module with specific curricular goals? If so, which ones?
7. Any other information you wish to share about your work.

#### Parts 3 and 4 (25%): Synchronous presentations (5%) and submission of proof of concept artifact (20%)

Near the end of the term, we will be reserving time for each group to synchronously present (5%) their work. In your presentation, take the audience through your project, including a "live" demonstration of your proof-of-concept learning module where you walk us through its subject matter and highlight some of its features while speaking to the ways in which you have integrated GenAI into the design and development process. Also go over other relevant experiences of the work, including the design and building process, encountering and overcoming challenges, and key takeaways. Some things to consider as you are putting your presentations together are: have your views on AI/GenAI and its use in teaching and learning shifted as you have worked through this assignment and engaged with the other work on the course? How have you tried to ensure that your use of GenAI in your project is thoughtful, careful, and considerate of concerns related to bias, blind spots, and ethics?

Use GenAI to devise a proof-of-concept learning module (20%) that is centered around a specific subject and learner cohort. You will decide which subject and target user you wish to develop this module for, and which GenAI tool(s) you will use to assist in design and development. Develop a proof-of-concept learning module following the plan you lay out in your proposal. The idea here is not a polished, fully functioning learning module, but rather a good faith effort at trial and error and experimentation with the topic(s), platform(s), and GenAI tools. We want to acknowledge here that we are not expecting expertise or precision; this project is intended to be productive, thoughtful, and fun collaborative work that gets us thinking about and working with GenAI in a hands-on fashion. The proof-of-concept artifact will be submitted following our synchronous presentations.

#### Part 5 (5%): Heuristic

Rather than a traditional self-reflection, this short assignment asks you to do just a *little more thinking* about your design artifact and, using what you learned in IP 2, develop a brief heuristic as a group that you think will work well to evaluate your artifact, what it does, how it works, and how well it accomplishes the task(s) you have designed it for. You can follow a similar sequence as you did in IP 2, without the need to invent a "use case" this time, given that you undoubtedly have a use case in mind for your designed artifact. This can and should be a BRIEF evaluation framework, so just 500~ words or so, and it should be completed together, as a group (consider for example a shared google doc or using word sharepoint, a miro board etc. for your ideation).

### Option B: Refusal

> *Most would never act upon such urges, but plenty have imagined what it would be like, how it would feel in the bones, to destroy a technological object that, momentarily or lastingly, appears hostile to one's ability to endure, let alone to flourish. Such dark dreams abound today, for technological modernization has proven much too poisonous, and much too boring, for centuries-old technological optimism to still impress many; not least, modern technology's solidification into the oligarchic power of "Big Tech," its mass harnessing of attention, its hypercommodification of social relations, its islands of e-waste, its unforeseen powers of surveillance and policing, and its proletarianization of work forces.* Dekeyser (2026), 2.

> *"The data, the applications, the algorithms, the cloud storage, the mobile devices, and the wearables we might disdain are the same ones that - in empowering much of our personal, social, and working lives - constitute us.* ***The imaginative challenge for contemporary technological politics is: How to refuse what is a part of you?"*** - Dekeyser (2026), 5.

In this course, we have encountered AI/GenAI along many axes and intersections, considering for example how AI has come to encroach upon our schools, our communities, our cities, our national and global supply chains/economics, our politics, and our most tightly-guarded notions of what it means to be in the world (and of the world). We have, admittedly, done this rather haphazardly, recognizing that it is impossible to spend much time in any one place, while still trying to gather breadth and a general understanding of some key changes AI/GenAI has and continues to make to the way that we organize thinking, teaching, and learning. Throughout this process, you have been asked to experiment with GenAI tools - to play with, analyze, interpret, and critique them. For option B of this project work, you are asked to do something different, working independently or with others (for this option), and that is to come up with your own scheme or framework for **refusal**. A first step here is to think about your own personal circumstances: Is your employer pressuring or *requiring* you to use GenAI as part of your job? Is refusal an option that is currently available to you? Building from this (and again, where you start is going to depend a little here), this project tasks you with coming up with some kind of framework, or guide, or manifesto, that is grounded, actionable, and purposeful, and which outlines a process and an informed rationalization for the refusal of GenAI in a specific context. What would a clear, concise and coherent policy for refusing the use of GenAI in a grade 4 English or science classroom look like? How about a policy for refusal amongst adult ELL learners? First-year university students taking general Ed courses? HR employees at a medium-sized software company? How, to paraphrase the quote included above, might we mobilize the refusal of that which is, increasingly, a part of us? This is not refusal as an expression of technophobia, but rather as a generative defence of the *human*. Below are a few recent articles that can help you get started in thinking about what this can look like.

- Jones, A., II. (2026, September 1). New York City public schools banning AI in middle schools. ABC News. <https://abcnews.com/Technology/new-york-city-public-schools-banning-ai-middle/story?id=136134872>
- Rosario, A. (2025, September 3). *I'm a high schooler. AI is demolishing my education.* The Atlantic. <https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/archive/2025/09/high-school-student-ai-education/684088>
- Winter, J. (2026, April 23). *What will it take to get A.I. out of schools?* The New Yorker. <https://www.newyorker.com/culture/progress-report/what-will-it-take-to-get-ai-out-of-schools>
- EU AI Policy: <https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/high-level-summary/>

Feedback will be detailed, personalized, and extensive, and grades for each assignment will adhere to the following scale.

It shows the individual (or group) has gone well beyond what has been provided and has extended the usual ways of thinking and/or performing. ### A Note on A+

An A+ is intended to be a rare grade, and while it is fine to consider it as a goal, it is important to be aware that consistently achieving this mark is rare in any course. Receiving an A on an assignment is not a sign that something has gone wrong, or that your work is limited in some way; rather, it means you have achieved the parameters of the assignment with great success. By contrast, an A+ will do this as well, but will also demonstrate a level of criticality and originality that is not expected or anticipated. That is, it will offer insight that does not make itself easily clear in the text(s) engaged for the assignment.
